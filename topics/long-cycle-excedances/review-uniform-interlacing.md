# Independent review of the full uniform long-cycle overlap

An independent research agent checked the intermediate- and large-threshold arguments in [the uniform proof](uniform-interlacing.md) against the exact excedance recurrence and the previously reviewed fixed-threshold and extreme-rank arguments. I find that the two notes together give an absolute existential $N$ such that, for every $n\ge N$ and $3\le k\le n$, both rows at the same $k$ have simple negative nonzero roots and strictly interlace after division by $t$. Zero is simple in both rows. The analytic proof reduces the original conjecture to a finite collection of rows and pairs. It gives no cutoff usable for finishing that collection. [Original conjecture](https://www.symmetricfunctions.com/realRootedOpenProblems.htm#conjLongCycleExcedances) · [Reviewed extreme-root proof](https://github.com/ProgramComputer/open-math-research/pull/7)

## Run-color comparison and its conditional law

1. The normalization is $a_{n,p}/p^n$. When every color appears in both halves every run boundary points up, so the failure probability on passing from size-only acceptance to full acceptance is at most $2p e^{-\lfloor n/2\rfloor/p}$. In the whole prescribed color band this is negligible even after multiplication by a polynomial in $n$.
2. Put $a=kp/n$ and $d=Dn/p$. The lower-window and upper-window bounds respectively follow from the binomial upper-tail exponent $(k-\mu)^2/(2k)$ and lower-tail exponent $(\mu-k)^2/(2\mu)$. In the point-mass estimate with value $k-1$, use $d-1$ in the first of these bounds. Increasing the absolute multiplier in $D$ suffices since $d\ge Bn/p$. In the $d\le k$ range $d\ge B\sqrt{k\log n}$ supplies the logarithm, and for $d>k$ the exponent is of order $d\gg\log n$. The number of colors is at most $b\sqrt n$, so $D=o(p)$ uniformly. The threshold squeeze and bounds for $d(p,L)$ follow by conditioning on the cycle lengths. For the upper estimate when $k$ is any number up to the word length, only the lower cutoff $L_-$ is needed. When $L_->p$, the permutation event is empty, and the same bound still holds. Thus the upper estimate for the residual problem does not need a second $11/20$ assumption.
3. For $a\to\infty$, $D/a=o(1)$ and all thresholds compared are bounded away from $p$ as a proportion. In the threshold increment estimate, excluding $j=p$ really is necessary because $d(0,L)=1$, and it is satisfied here by $a\le11p/20$. Insertion into an existing valid permutation has $p$ choices, while the only invalid configuration it can cure has exactly one cycle of length $L-1$ with all other cycles accepted. The expected count of such cycles times the insertion choices gives the second term of (6). Relative change is $O(1/p)$ using the two-sided bounds on $d(p,L)$. For bounded $a$, the independent recoloring gives a uniform $(p+1)$-color word and the insertion gives a uniform $(p+1)$-color permutation. Outside the rare event of modifying small cycles structurally, those cycles only lose labels. Their expected loss is $O_J(\log n)$, their old count has a point-mass bound $O_J(\sqrt{p/n})$, and summation over their expected $O_J(1)$ count gives a vanishing probability. The bounded and unbounded subsequence argument establishes the uniform ratio.
4. For the consecutive-size increment, fix a marked cycle of $j<p$ colors. Its expected count $1/j$ and the new label landing in it with probability $j/p$ produce exactly $1/p$. Conditional on its old occupancy $k-1$, and given its set of colors, the remaining permutation on the other colors is uniform and independent of the residual word. The $n-(k-1)$ old labels outside the marked colors are independent uniform residual colors after conditioning also on the label positions. Dropping that position condition does not affect their cycle-size counts. All other cycles must already exceed the length cutoff for acceptance to change, since the new label is in the marked cycle. This gives (8) with residual word length $n-k+1$ and color count $p-j$, and with the same minimum length $k$. The all-color cycle contributes zero to the increment because $n\ge k$.
5. On $|j-a|\le D$, both residual indices stay comparable to the originals: $p-j\ge(9/20-o(1))p$ and $n-k+1\ge9n/20$. Therefore the residual color band has only constant changes in its endpoints, and $k(p-j)/(n-k+1)\asymp a$. If $k>n-k+1$ the probability is zero. Otherwise the one-sided threshold squeeze above bounds it by $O((1+a)^{-1})=O(\alpha_{n,p})$. This step is relative, which is essential when $a$ grows. The beta integral and the supremum of the unimodal binomial probability kernel bound the lattice sum by $O(k^{-1/2}+p/n)$. After the factor $1/p$ this is $o(1/p)$ relatively when $k\to\infty$. Boundary encoding errors remain negligible compared with $\alpha_{n,p}/p$, and bounded $k$ is covered by the all-colors Chernoff event.

On the front annulus, uniformly bounded coefficient ratios for $h/2\le p\le2h$ and convergence to one at every fixed distance from $h$ suffice by dominated geometric summation. Outside this band even a polynomial loss is negligible. Fixed-$k$ geometric margins therefore persist. The consecutive-size increment bound applies to all terms in the central band, while the negative $p=h+1$ term is bounded away from zero. At an old rank-$h$ root the new value of $E_{n+1,k}/t$ has sign $(-1)^h$. The old modulus lies strictly inside the next row's rank-$h$ annulus, giving the absolute ordering. Counting smaller-modulus sign factors is legitimate even before the other roots are known to be real, because nonreal zeros occur in conjugate pairs and the polynomial has positive coefficients. The front ranges overlap the earlier uniform ranks by choosing the positive constant in their lower bound smaller than $1/4$.

## Intermediate pole analysis

The logarithm identity (11) holds at the odd poles with the sign written. For comparing poles vertically one should explicitly call $\sum_m Q(k,mw)/m$ an auxiliary analytic sum on $\Re w>0$, since (11) itself is stated only at the poles. Its derivative is the negative gamma-density sum written in (14). The derivative of its negative, as used in $\log T_s(w_p)$, has the opposite sign, which is irrelevant to the modulus estimate. The sum evaluated at the real point $w=s$ is real, and along $w_1=s+i\pi$ the additional derivative of $-\log(1+e^{-s})$ is also real.

The numerator disk bound (12) has the claimed coefficient 20 on $\log k$. For $s\le10$ it follows from the summable squared pole ratios and the harmonic sum, with the enlargement $e^{2A\ell/k}$ costing only $O_A(1)$ beyond that harmonic sum. In the next range, $R_{\max}-s\le6/s$ follows from $r-s\le\pi^2/(2s)$ and $Ac_1^2<1/4$. For $m>4s$ the Poisson mean exceeds $4s^2$ and the exponent loss is at most $6m/s-m s/8\le-ms/20$ since $s\ge10$. The upper-degree pole part has summable ratios bounded by three. For $c_1\sqrt k\le s\le10k$, the first $M$ terms cost $\log(1+k/s)+O_{A,c_1}(1)$ because $(R_{\max}-s)M$ is bounded. The tail has the same exponential smallness since $(R_{\max}-s)/s=O_{A,c_1}(k^{-1})$. If $s\ge10k$ all Poisson means are in the tail from the first term.

The near-pole contour excludes all other poles when $s\le c_1\sqrt k$. Indeed $|w_3|/r-1\ge2\pi^2/r^2$, enough for the stated margin on choosing $c_1$ small. For the remaining $s$, $O(\sqrt n)$ forbidden intervals of width $2n^{-2}$ leave an admissible radius in the candidate interval. The factor $r n^2 k^{40}$ costs at most $O(n^{42.5})$. Since $A/(2C_0)>50$ and $k\to\infty$, (13) is uniformly $o(S)$. The old and new row can use the same contour.

For the vertical derivative, $|\Im w_p|=O_{A,c_1}(s/\sqrt k)$ for every included pole and every point on its segment, so $(|w|/s)^{k-1}$ is bounded. The gamma-density sampled sum is $O(k^{-1/2}+1/s)$ by its integral and its maximum, at most $O_{c_1}(k^{-1/2})$ on this range. Consequently included amplitudes are uniformly bounded relative to the principal one, and their ratios tend to one at every fixed included pole. The weighted pole envelope is independent of $k,n$ and summable when $nS^2\ge1$. This condition holds on $s\le3\sqrt n$ for large $n$ because $\pi>3$. Split at a fixed pole index after fixing $A,c_1$ to obtain the required absolute far-pole margin. The old-to-new subtraction costs $pS$ per pole, so the relative error in the derivative-like expression retains the factor $S$.

For the low-$s$ phase derivative, the $p=1$ term is constant and $p=-1$ is a geometric sum of bound $O(1/S)$. For the remaining terms use $|v_p'|=|p-1||v_p|^2$ and sum the geometric moduli. This gives $O(|p|^{-1}S^{-2}(1+c p^2S^2)^{-1/2})$ for each pole. Their sum is $O((1+s)^2\log(2+s))$ with an absolute constant independent of $c_1$. The choice of small $c_1$ then makes it less than half the main derivative for $k\le C_0 n/\log n$. In the upper $s$ range differentiating along $w_1=s+i\pi$ using (14) gives $o(1)$ in $s$, while the main phase derivative has a positive lower bound in magnitude on $s\le3\sqrt n$.

The front bracket comparison at $h_* =\lfloor7\sqrt n/20\rfloor$ remains valid uniformly because both $h_*$ and $h_*+1$ are in the front range for large $n$. The old phase shifts by $o(1)$ at $s\asymp\sqrt n$ in this range of $k$. The count below modulus one is $\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1$, and for odd $n$ reciprocity supplies the one further root at $-1$. Exhausting the degree fixes the rank in each phase interval. The same weighted difference yields the strict next-row signs on these roots. A potentially violating sequence has either a subsequence with $k\to\infty$ or a subsequence with a single fixed $k$, the latter already covered. This proves uniformity over the whole intermediate range.

## High-$k$ derivative and the final overlap

Here $D=n/k\le\log n/100$. Taking $k$ large, the absolute pole bounds for $P$ and $P_1$ follow for $p\ge3$ by expanding one quadratic-factor Bernoulli bound into the fourth-power majorant. The resulting $P=O(1+\sqrt D)$ and $P_1=O(1+D)$ give $e^{2\sqrt{PD}}=n^{o(1)}$. To differentiate the mixed-pole expansion, differentiate each original summand before summation by parts. When the derivative is on a designated pair, only one of $1/a$, $1/(b-a)$ remains, and it is monotone with supremum plus variation at most $2/k\le 2n/k^2$. The geometric cancellation and the factor $|z_p z_r|^{k-1}$ are unchanged. A derivative elsewhere costs at most $n(1+|p_i|)$, and replacing one pole sum by $P_1$ accounts for the extra weight. The same partial-sum injection for length vectors and factorials from the cycle EGF give the stated bound $n^{-1/2+o(1)}$. This is $o(nd_n)$ since $nd_n\ge1$. The undifferentiated bound is $o(d_n)$. The single-pole nonprincipal error follows from $|z_p'|\le |p||z_p|^2$ and $p|z_p|\ge1$ for $p\ge1$.

At an old root $t\,d\theta/dt=\pi/r^2$ gives exactly the factor $S$ on differentiating the normalized row. The lag term has prefactor $r^k c_k(t)/((k-1)!(1-t)^k)=\sum_p z_p^k$. If its residual size $m=n+1-k$ is below $k$, that term vanishes. Otherwise $m\le n$ and the upper bound on compositions and absolute pole sums gives the stated estimate, $o(nd_n S)$, using $S\ge n^{-1/2}$. The resulting strict sign is the negative sine of $n\theta$. Both old and new rows keep the same $k$ throughout.

To make the final overlap explicit, take $C_0=100$. For $100n/\log n\le k\le11n/20$ use the front theorem and the high-$k$ integer phase samples $\theta_j=j\pi/n$, starting with $j=H+1$ for $H=\lfloor3\sqrt n/8\rfloor$. The first sample obeys $nS^2\ge1$ uniformly, and $s\le n/(H+1)$ there. The fixed-threshold logarithmic separator even with $(n+1)/(H+1)$ puts the first $H$ roots before it. The same degree, center-parity, and reciprocity argument then gives the high-$k$ absolute counts and consecutive signs. If $k>11n/20$, for large $n$ both $n$ and $n+1$ are strictly below $2k$, so each row is the single-cycle Eulerian case. These ranges overlap without changing $k$ inside any pair. The full theorem is an eventual uniform result and does not imply the remaining finite conjecture without additional work.
