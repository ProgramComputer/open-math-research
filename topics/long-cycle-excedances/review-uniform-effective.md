# Independent review of the numerical uniform cutoff

I independently checked [effective-uniform.md](effective-uniform.md), its common contour, and its join to [the effective front argument](effective-front.md) and [the large-threshold argument](separated-poles.md). I found no substantive gap. The proof gives simple negative nonzero roots and strict interlacing of the nonzero roots of $E_{n,k}$ and $E_{n+1,k}$ for every $n\ge10^{20}$ and $3\le k\le n$. I also checked the stronger contour criterion with $K=k$, valid for $n\ge10^6$ and $n/(200k)>30\log n+20$. The checked adjacent sign applies to the actual next row. There is no checked Lean or Rocq proof.

The exact original conjecture asks for real-rootedness and consecutive interlacing for each $k\ge3$ at all sizes $n\ge k$. Removing the common simple zero gives the strict interlacing stated here. The cutoff leaves sizes below $10^{20}$ only partly covered. This is an agent-assisted review of a partial theorem. [Alexandersson’s 2026 primary statement](https://www.symmetricfunctions.com/realRootedOpenProblems.htm#conjLongCycleExcedances)

## Coefficients and logarithm bound

- The ordinary Eulerian power-sum identity gives the unrestricted artificial cycle series $\sum_{\ell\ge1} tA_{\ell-1}(t)w^\ell/[\ell!(1-t)^\ell]=\log((1-t)/(1-t e^w))$ near $w=0$. Only cycle lengths $\ell\ge k$ enter the restricted polynomial, so subtracting the terms with $1\le\ell<k$ gives the displayed $T_s(w)/(1+e^{w-s})$ after dividing the generating function by $1-t$. Its logarithm $G_s$ is the negative of that finite polynomial, hence is single valued, with $G_s(0)=0$ and linear term $-t w/(1-t)$. The usual Eulerian pole identity gives the terms of degree $\ell\ge2$. For $s>0$, expanding the logarithm at $w=0$ gives the absolutely convergent sum over positive integers $a$. At $s=0$ the finite coefficients extend by continuity. This normalization uses the same $k$ and $t$ in both rows.
- For $s\le10$, $\rho_p=|w_1/w_p|\le1$ and $\sum_{p\ge3,\ p\text{ odd}}1/(p^2-1)=1/4$. Thus $\sum_p\rho_p^\ell\le\sum_p\rho_p^2\le2+1/(2S^2)<8$ for $\ell\ge2$. The linear contribution is below six because $e^{-s}/(1+e^{-s})\le1/2$. All remaining terms cost less than $10\log k$ using $\sum_{\ell=2}^{k-1}1/\ell\le\log k$ and $(R_*/r)^{k-1}\le e^{1/50}$.
- For $s>10$, $r-s<5/s$, so $\delta=R_*-s\le5/s+s/(40k)$. Taking absolute values of the terms through degree $L=\min(k-1,\lfloor s^2/16\rfloor)$ bounds the contribution from a single $a$ by $e^{a\delta}\Pr\{\operatorname{Pois}(aR_*)\le L\}/a$, where including the constant term enlarges the bound. For $a\le2L/s$, $a\delta\le5/8+1/20<1$ and $2L/s<k$, so this sum costs at most $3(1+\log k)$. For $a>2L/s$, the lower-tail Chernoff bound is $e^{-aR_*/8}$. Now $\delta/s<3/50<1/14$, so $e^{a\delta-aR_*/8}\le e^{-as/16}$. The sum of these terms costs less than two. If any higher degrees remain, they satisfy $\ell>s^2/16$, giving $\ell\ge7$ and $\ell S^2>1/2$. The associated lattice estimate is valid for $\ell\ge4$ by Bernoulli's inequality and the spacing-two integral comparison. It bounds $\sum_p\rho_p^\ell<5$. The remaining degrees cost at most $6\log k$. Together these give $|G_s(w)|\le B=6+10\log k$ on $|w|\le R_*$. Writing the explicit factor $e^{a\delta}$ before applying the Poisson bound makes this step easier to audit.

## Common contour and adjacent residues

For $s\le10\sqrt k$, the estimate $R_*^2-r^2\le(100+10/3)(1/25+1/7500)<5$ holds for every $k\ge3$. The first nonprincipal radius has squared gap $8\pi^2$, so the smaller circle encloses only the principal pair. It stays more than $n^{-2}$ from them by $r/(100k)\ge\pi/(100n)$. The next radial distance is at least $(8\pi^2-5)/(4\pi\sqrt n)>n^{-2}$, since $r\le\pi\sqrt n$ under $nS^2\ge1$. For $s>10\sqrt k$, the admissible interval has length at least $\pi/(100n)$. Every relevant pole radius is below $2\pi\sqrt n$, so fewer than $10\sqrt n$ radii can remove intervals of length $2n^{-2}$ each. Already at $n=10^6$, $20n^{-3/2}<\pi/(100n)$. Thus a common radius exists. The distance of each point on the circle from each pole is at least the radial distance. Writing $d=n^{-2}$, if $|\Re(w-s)|\ge d/2$ the denominator is at least $1-e^{-d/2}\ge d/4$. Otherwise the imaginary distance to the nearest odd multiple of $\pi$ is at least $\sqrt3d/2$. Applying $\sin y\ge2y/\pi$ on $[0,\pi/2]$ in the squared-modulus formula gives the same result. Both bounds imply $|1+e^{w-s}|^{-1}\le4n^2$.

The generating function has residue $-T_s(w_p)$. Moving the initial Cauchy contour outward therefore contributes $+T_s(w_p)/w_p^{j+1}$ to the coefficient. Pairing complex conjugates cancels the factor two in the definition of $Y_j$. The remaining circle bound is $2n^2r e^{2B}(r/R)^j$ with $j=n,n+1$. Since $\log(R/r)\ge1/(200k)$ and $|T_s(w_1)|\ge e^{-B}$, the stated criterion bounds the error above by $2\pi e^{-8} n^{-15/2}<n^{-2}$. In the difference of the two rows the circle errors cost at most $2n^{-2}$, which fits under $(1/2-21/50)S$ for $n\ge10^6$ and $nS^2\ge1$.

At an odd pole, the missing degree-zero term of the exponential series equals $-\log(1+e^{-s})$, giving the stated expression involving $H(w_p)$. Differentiating the entire finite inner sum cancels its lower powers and leaves $-\sum_{a\ge1}e^{-aw}(aw)^{k-1}/(k-1)!$. Uniform convergence on each closed vertical segment follows from the finite-degree summand and $s>0$. On these segments, $\log(|w|/s)\le\log(R_*/s)<7/(100k)$, using $s>10\sqrt k$. The absolute derivative is consequently bounded by $e^{7/100}$ times the indicated sampled gamma density. This density integrates to one. By unimodality, its samples at multiples of $s$ have sum at most $1/s$ plus the maximum, via adjacent intervals of length $s$. For $k\ge14$, Stirling bounds the maximum by $1/\sqrt{2\pi(k-1)}$. Thus

$$
\pi|H'|\le\pi e^{7/100}\left(\frac1{10\sqrt{14}}+\frac1{\sqrt{26\pi}}\right)<\frac{12}{25}.
$$

For $3\le k\le13$, $s>10\sqrt k\ge2k$ and $s>17$. The sample at $as$ is at most its Poisson half-mean lower-tail probability $e^{-as/8}$. Summing bounds $\pi|H'|$ by $\pi e^{7/100}/(e^{17/8}-1)<12/25$. These arguments verify the stronger $K=k$ version. They also imply the original $K=\max(k,10000)$ estimate, since its large-$s$ regime is smaller and the same vertical relative-increase bound holds.

Integration along the vertical segments gives $|\gamma|<12/25$ and the stated difference bound with $p+1$. If a pole is enclosed, $u=(p^2-1)S^2\le1/(20k)$, so $\log(1+u)\ge u/(1+u)$ and $nS^2\ge1$ supply decay $\exp(-(49/100)(p^2-1))$. The exponent combined with the numerator difference is at most $-2$ at $p=3$ and at most $-(37/100)(p^2-1)$ for $p\ge5$. Summing the first term and half the weighted tail integral from $5$ gives the bound $21/50$. Finally $z_1=e^{-i\theta}$ and $z_1-\cos\theta=-iS$, so the principal term in $Y_{n+1}-\cos\theta\,Y_n$ is $-S\sin\Psi$. For the other poles, $|z_p-\cos\theta|\le pS$ follows by squaring the expression using $z_p=(\cos\theta+ipS)^{-1}$. This proves both margins in (3) with the required sign and scale.

## Phase hitting, reflection, and cutoff

The front theorem supplies $H_0$ distinct simple negative roots below $|t(\theta_*)|$ and the appropriate signs at them. For the join, $H_0+5/6>(17/50)\sqrt n$ and $H_0+1\le(9/25)\sqrt n$ at $n\ge10^6$. The Taylor bounds give $n\sin^2\theta_*>1$ and $s_*>2\sqrt n$. The criterion gives $n/k>25$ and consequently $s_*>10\sqrt k$. The lower bound on the logarithmic front separation follows from $-\log(1-x)\ge x+x^2/2$ at $x=1/(H_0+1)$. Its scalar coefficient is $1/3-1/[36(H_0+5/6)]>33/100$. In particular $\theta_*<1/100$ and $|\gamma(\theta_*)|+\theta_*<\pi/6$, which puts the phase strictly below $(H_0+1)\pi$. At the other endpoint, odd powers of degree at least three vanish in the exponent by pairing opposite poles at $s=0$. The even powers are real and the linear term at $w_1=i\pi$ has imaginary part $\pi/2$. Thus $\Psi(\pi/2)=n\pi/2$ exactly. The phase is continuous because $G_s$ is the specified polynomial evaluated at $s+i\pi$.

For each consecutive pair of phase levels, use the last hit of the lower level before the first hit of the upper level. These hits exist by continuity and compactness. Between them the phase is in the open level interval, since front-to-back traversal starts below the lower level and the upper level has never previously been hit. The open intervals are disjoint and ordered. Equation (3) gives opposite old endpoint signs and, at any old zero selected inside, $|\cos\Psi|<1/2$. Therefore $Y_{n+1}$ has sign $-\operatorname{sgn}\sin\Psi$. For the lower-half gap with phase between $j\pi$ and $(j+1)\pi$, this is $(-1)^{j+1}$ for $E_{n+1,k}$ and $(-1)^j$ for $P_{n+1}$. Division by negative $t$ gives the sign change.

Write $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. Both parities give $m-H_0-1$ lower-half gaps. For even $n$, reflecting the selected roots and adding the $2H_0$ front roots yields $2m-2=n-2$ distinct old roots. If the first hit of the endpoint level is early, the selected roots still lie strictly on the lower half, and the degree count accounts for all roots. For odd $n$, reciprocal symmetry gives the additional root $-1$. Its phase $(m+1/2)\pi$ gives sign $(-1)^m$ for $P_{n+1}(-1)$. The count is now $2m-1=n-2$. In both cases the counts exhaust the degree and make every old root simple. At reciprocal roots the old-root rank changes from $h$ to $n-1-h$, and $P_{n+1}(1/x)=x^{-(n-1)}P_{n+1}(x)$ gives exactly the required sign at the reflected rank. The front signs from the insertion comparison complete the alternating signs. The new row has constant and leading coefficients one and degree $n-1$, so the two outer intervals and every gap each contain exactly one simple negative root.

To compose this contour criterion with the large-threshold theorem, consider $n\ge10^{20}$. When $k\le\sqrt n$, the left side of the criterion is at least $\sqrt n/200$. Otherwise, outside the large-threshold theorem, $k<2000\sqrt n(3+\log3+(\log n)/4)$. With $L=\log n\ge40$, the logarithmic derivative in $L$ of $\sqrt n/[(3+\log3+L/4)(30L+20)]$ is at least $1/2-2/L>0$. The same argument with one factor covers $k\le\sqrt n$. At $n=10^{20}$ the scalar bounds follow from $\log3<6/5$ and $\log10<7/3$. Thus the two criteria cover all $k$ at the claimed uniform cutoff.
