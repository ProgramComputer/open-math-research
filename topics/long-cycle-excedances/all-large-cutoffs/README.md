# A proposed proof for every minimum cycle length at least seven

For integers $`n\ge k\ge7`$, let $`E_{n,k}(t)`$ count permutations of $`[n]`$ with every cycle of length at least $`k`$, weighted by $`t`$ to the number of strict excedances. The proposed theorem asserts that the zeros of $`E_{n,k}(t)/t`$ and $`E_{n+1,k}(t)/t`$ are simple, strictly negative and strictly interlace. The larger row supplies both outer zeros. This is the part of Alexandersson’s excedance conjecture with $`k\ge7`$. A fresh-context blind referee found no substantive gap in the argument checked and independently replayed the complete second finite method. The report specifies which earlier computations were sampled. [Blind referee report](blind-review.md) **Formal verification**　🟨 Partial · Lean 4. Lean proves that the old-root and new-row sign witnesses give the complete interlacing root lists. Constructing those witnesses from the actual permutation coefficients and the analytic estimates remains outside the checked theorem. ## Proof by ranges

Write $`x=n/k`$. Inverting the permutation takes an excedance to a deficiency and preserves its cycle lengths. Since there are no fixed points, its inverse has $`n-\mathrm{exc}(\pi)`$ excedances. A permutation with one excedance is a single cycle with one ascent, hence is unique. Its inverse gives the unique permutation with $`n-1`$ excedances. Thus $`P_n=E_{n,k}/t`$ is reciprocal of degree $`n-2`$, with constant and leading coefficients one. The original rows share a simple zero at the origin.

The inequalities in the first column apply to the old size. Each ingredient compares it with $`n+1`$ at the same $`k`$, including when the new size crosses a boundary of its row in the table. In the last regime the finite calculation also includes $`n=1{,}000`$ as an overlap. The two implementations tested 93,902 pairs. At 82,395 of them an intermediate gap remained after the two angular estimates. The programs checked all 541,199 such gaps.

For this join, put $`H=\lfloor7\sqrt n/20\rfloor`$. The exterior certificate constructs $`H`$ roots of modulus below the first integer sample $`(H+1)\pi/n`$. Let $`J\ge H+1`$ be the first certified integer angular sample for the high-angle comparison. The finite brackets give a root for each gap indexed $`H+1`$ through $`J-1`$. The high-angle argument gives a root in each gap indexed $`J`$ through $`\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1`$. These give $`\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1`$ distinct roots of modulus below one. Reciprocity yields the same number of roots of modulus above one. For odd $`n`$, it also gives the root at $`-1`$. The count is exactly $`n-2`$, which proves simplicity and exhausts the old degree. The high-angle sign at $`-1`$ supplies the central next-row sign in the odd case. Alternating next-row signs at the old roots and the endpoint signs of $`P_{n+1}`$ give exactly $`n-1`$ distinct intervening negative zeros. The independent finite checker proves the same conclusion by placing the new roots in the complementary gaps between old-root brackets. [Signs and endpoint proof](../intermediate/README.md)
