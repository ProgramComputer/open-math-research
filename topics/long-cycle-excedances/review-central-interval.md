# Independent review of the all-cycle central interval

I verified the argument in [central-interval.md](central-interval.md) and the discrete estimates in [cycle-probability-variation.md](cycle-probability-variation.md). This agent-assisted review supports the following local conclusion for every $n\ge k\ge3$. If $k\sin^2\theta\ge8$, then $F_n$ has sign $(-1)^l$ at each sample $\theta=l\pi/n$. At every old root in the same interval, the root is simple and $|L|<|t(1-t)P_n'|$. It holds at the closed endpoints, including the singleton interval at $k=8$. These estimates supply local root and insertion signs. A complete interlacing proof still needs to locate and compare the roots outside the interval.

**Principal factors.** The recurrence gives $d_m=1/m$ for $k\le m<2k$, and the inductive lower bound $1/(2k-1)$ uses $1+(m-2k+1)/(2k-1)=m/(2k-1)$. The tail variation charges each $\Delta_i$ at most $k-1$ times. The last strict rational comparison has positive difference $6k^2-k+1$ after clearing denominators. Extending by zero introduces a jump of size $1/k$. The product weights therefore have variation at most $4/k^2$. Multiplying by $(n-2j)/n$ adds at most $2/k^2$, with the two support transitions still within $0\le j\le n$. Division by $|1-e^{-2i\theta}|=2\sin\theta$ gives both stated bounds. I also checked the fixed mixed-pole estimate by extracting $(z_p/z_a)^k$ before applying the same difference identity. The two absolute-mass examples have the claimed divergent ratios.

**Other factors and differentiation.** Comparing $(1+8(p^2-1)/k)^k$ at $k$ and at 8 gives $\rho_p^k\le|p|^{-8}$. For the far poles, $(p^2-1)S^2\ge64/k$, so the factor $1/[k(1-\rho_p)]$ is at most $2/k+1/32<1$. The summable $\varepsilon$ bound then applies to both the mass and the supremum estimates. At total degree $j$, write $m_p$ for the degree drawn from the factor at pole $p$. A derivative of the term divided by $j$ costs at most $\sum_{p:m_p>0}|p|m_p/j$, which is at most $\sum_{p:m_p>0}|p|$. This makes it legitimate to charge each occupied pole and use the same fixed-index supremum as for undifferentiated terms. For the total bound over $j$ the charged pole contributes its positive-index mass $a_p$. For a fixed $j$, that one factor contributes its positive-index supremum $\rho_p^k/k$, since once the remaining indices have been chosen, its index is determined. The other factors contribute absolute masses in both estimates. This distinction gives $2\varepsilon$ for the sums and $2\varepsilon/k$ for the coefficientwise suprema, for both $h_j$ and $h'_j/j$. Termwise differentiation is valid locally on $0<\theta<\pi$, where the majorant for the differentiated nonconstant coefficients at a fixed degree is $O(|p|^{1-k})$. This includes a neighborhood of $\pi/2$.

**Convolution and constants.** The $j=n$ term is $h_n$ because $f_0=1$ and $f'_0=0$. It must use the supremum estimate, while the other terms use the total estimates and $|f_m|,|f'_m|/m<3/k$ for $m\ge k$. The intermediate indices below $k$ vanish. The derivative weights $j/n$ and $(n-j)/n$ account for the two summands of the derivative. The resulting constants are $8\varepsilon/k$ for the value and $14\varepsilon/k$ for the derivative divided by $n$. Since $2/(kS)+8\varepsilon<3/10$, the error at a sample is smaller than the principal amplitude and $|\cos(n\theta)|<3/10$ at an old zero. There $|\sin(n\theta)|>19/20$ and $3/(kS)+14\varepsilon<9/20$, giving $|F'_n|>n/(2k)$ and $`\mathrm{sgn}\, F'_n=-\mathrm{sgn}\,\sin(n\theta)`$. I checked these elementary margins with rational arithmetic.

**Common normalization and orientation.** Put $m=n+1-k$ and multiply the insertion identity at an old root by $t r^{n+1}/(n!(1-t)^{n+1})$. The derivative becomes $S F_n'$ by $t\,d\theta/dt=\pi/r^2$. The lag becomes $B_kF_m$ because $\binom n{k-1}/n!=1/((k-1)!m!)$, with $B_k$ the normalized enumerator of one $k$-cycle. The residual enumerator in $F_m$ can contain any number of cycles. This multiplier is negative, while its product with $P_{n+1}$ is $(n+1)F_{n+1}$. Thus the normalized next row has derivative sign, and $P_{n+1}$ has the sign of $t(1-t)P_n'$. For a nonzero lag the index inequality gives $nS\ge15$, so the derivative magnitude exceeds $15/(2k)$ and the lag is less than $5/k$. Reciprocity scales both old-root terms by the same factor $t^{1-n}$ on reflection. It also gives $F_n(\pi-\theta)=(-1)^nF_n(\theta)$, preserving the integer-sample signs on the reflected side.

At even $n$, $\pi/2$ is itself an integer sample. At odd $n$, reciprocity gives the root at $-1$ and $|\sin(n\pi/2)|=1$. I checked both boundary cases at $k=8$ by reconstructing the exact values and derivatives at $-1$ from the cycle containing label one. At $n=32$ the central sample has positive sign. At $n=33$ the normalized orientation holds with a nonzero lag and $|L/D|\approx0.06246$. The residual row there can have three cycles.

I found no substantive gap in the stated central interval. The result is a written argument with no checked Lean or Rocq proof.
