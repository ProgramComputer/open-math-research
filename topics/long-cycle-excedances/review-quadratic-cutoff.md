# Review of the uniform quadratic minimum-cycle cutoff

The estimates in [the uniform cutoff proof](quadratic-cutoff.md) prove the asserted existence of a single absolute $B$ for all $k\ge3$ and rows $n\ge Bk^2$. The conclusion includes strict consecutive interlacing of the nonzero roots at the **same** $k$ and covers $n+1$ automatically. I did not find a coefficient, exponent, or quantifier error. I would explicitly state where large $B$ gives $(n/2)S^2\ge1$, since the small-$s$ tail splitting requires this stronger inequality. The result is compatible with the conjecture's parameters. No particular numerical $B$ is certified by this argument.

- The signs in both expansions for $L(w)$ agree. For $s>0$, expand $\log(1+e^{w-s})$ as the usual logarithm series near $w=0$, whose degree-$j$ coefficient for $j\ge2$ equals the negative alternating Eulerian power sum divided by $j!$. Hadamard factorization gives that same coefficient as $-\sum_p w_p^{-j}/j$. The genus-one product converges locally after subtracting the constant and linear terms, because $\sum_p |w_p|^{-2}<\infty$. At $s=0$, the power-series expression remains well defined and the Hadamard identity follows directly from the factorization or by the limit $s\downarrow0$.

- For $s\ge10k$, the original circle has $R\le2s$. The nonlinear part and its $s$ derivative along $w_1$ are both $O(4^k e^{-s/2})$ uniformly. Differentiation either cancels the factor $1/m$ or supplies a new factor $m$ from $mw_1$. Both resulting exponentials use the same majorant. Since $1/2-(\log 4)/10>1/3$, these quantities are $O(e^{-s/3})$. The differentiated linear term is $O((1+s)e^{-s})$. The extra factor $O(1+s^2)$ for the angular derivative does not affect boundedness, and the phase itself retains exponential decay. The finitely many exceptional compact $k,s$ ranges can be absorbed by enlarging $B$, using the earlier fixed-$k$ estimates there.

- On $s\le10k$ there are at most $O(k)$ distinct pole radii below $3r$, and the interval of candidate radii has length $r/k\ge\pi/k$. Excluding intervals of radius $c/k^2$ leaves a candidate for a small absolute $c$. More remote poles lie still farther away. On the selected circle use the elementary denominator estimate with radial distance $d=c/k^2$. If $|\Re w-s|\ge d/2$, the modulus is bounded below by $1-e^{-d/2}\gg d$. Otherwise the imaginary distance to the nearest pole height is at least $\sqrt3d/2$ and the sine bound applies. Therefore the reciprocal is $O(k^2)$.

- In $s\le\sqrt k$, using $|w/r|^j\le(1+2/k)^k\le e^2$ proves the numerator bound in the note. The exact angular derivative is $|v_p'|=|p-1||v_p|^2$. For $j\ge2$, $|v_p|^{j-1}|v_p'|\le|p-1||v_p|^3$ since $|v_p|\le1$. Splitting at $|p|=1+s$ bounds the sum by $O((1+s)^2)$, and $k(1+s)^2=O(k^2)$ uniformly on this interval. The linear phase derivative is uniformly bounded.

- In $\sqrt k\le s\le10k$, $R-s\ll 1/s+s/k$. The product $(10k/s+1)(1/s+s/k)$ is bounded uniformly, using $k/s^2\le1$ and $s/k\le10$. The first $M=\lceil10k/s\rceil$ terms in the logarithm series have harmonic cost $O(1+\log^+(k/s))$. The tail estimate $2^k e^{-ms+mR/2}\le e^{-ms/8}$ holds for all sufficiently large $k$ because $R/s\le3/2$ and $ms>10k$. Under differentiation, the first $M$ terms cost $O(M)$ and the tail still has bounded geometric sum. Thus the angular derivative costs at most $O((1+s^2)(1+k/s))=O(k^2)$. All finitely many excluded $k$ are compatible with the same absolute estimate after increasing its constant.

- The circle error divided by $S$ has logarithmic bound $C(1+\sqrt k)\log(k+1)+O(\log k)-n/(2k)$, because $r=O(k)$ and $r/R\le(1+1/k)^{-1}$. The first terms are $O(k)$ uniformly over $k\ge3$, so increasing $B$ gives the required small relative margin for both $n$ and $n+1$. For the other poles in this range, $n\ge Bk^2$ and $s\le10k$ imply $(n/2)S^2\ge c_0B$ for an absolute $c_0>0$, verifying the half-exponent premise. In $s\le\sqrt k$, $n/(1+s^2)\gg Bk$ absorbs $(1+\sqrt k)\log(k+1)$. In $s\ge\sqrt k$, setting $x=k/s$ yields $n/(1+s^2)\gg Bx^2$, and $(1+\log^+ x)/x^2$ is bounded for $x\ge1/10$. Thus the principal-relative error for the old coefficient is smaller than $1/4$ and the one for the consecutive difference is smaller than $S/4$. In $s\ge10k$ one can use the full exponent's $1/10$ weighted pole sum on the original domain $nS^2\ge1$, together with the uniformly bounded amplitude ratios.

- The uniform $O(k^2)$ phase derivative permits a single $B$ that forces strict phase increase. At $s=10k$, the dominant phase grows at least as a positive constant times $n/k$, so the first target $\bigl(\lfloor3\sqrt n/8\rfloor+1\bigr)\pi$ is hit at larger $s$ when $B$ is large. Since there the phase correction is uniformly bounded, the first hit has $s\asymp\sqrt n$ and the correction is $O(e^{-c\sqrt n})$. This retains the sine and logarithmic rank-separator margins of the fixed-threshold argument. The small-rank proof independently needs only $n\ge\max(400,25k^2)$. The absolute ranks, reflection, and the signs at old roots then follow with all parameters in the prescribed range.
