# Blind referee report on the logarithmic dimension bound

**Judgment.** I find the main theorem mathematically sound. Accept as a partial theorem with the source and corollary clarifications below. It gives absolute, existential constants $C,N$ and proves exact real-rootedness with distinct negative zeros for all $n\ge N$, all $d\ge Cn/\log n$, and all caps $1\le r\le d$. The source uses the same labeled matrix counts and quota exponent and asks for real-rootedness for every $n\ge0$, $d\ge1$, and $1\le r\le d$. The unrestricted conjecture remains open. This was an agent-assisted independent review of an anonymized packet. [Original conjecture, Balanced arrays of uniform matroids](https://www.symmetricfunctions.com/realRootedOpenProblems.htm#conjBalancedArrays)

## Coefficient estimates and cited enumeration

I checked the cyclic construction for full support $0,\ldots,\lfloor nr/d\rfloor$, the mixed-quota inequality, and the normalized diagonal log-concavity. The within-column comparison swaps row caps. The cross-column comparison switches one forced dummy of degree one. The outdegree lower bound on the first product dominates the indegree lower bound, and their sum is preserved by the switches. Paired exclusive-cell partitions contribute the same multiplicity per common and exclusive skeleton on both sides. The binomial denominators cancel in the mixed inequality, and diagonal multiplication gives the claimed strict curvature. The ordered negative test points give all roots as soon as their signs alternate. A test point can fail only if its curvature ratio is at most four. [Kahn–Neiman, §4, Lemma 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1001.0610)

The weak-center bound follows from $\log(1+t)\ge t/(1+t)$ applied to the binomial curvature at the failed quota. This gives $k(n-k)/n^2\ge \delta/\log 4-O(1/n)$. Moving $O(\log^2 n)$ quotas changes this normalized product by $O(\log^2 n/n)=o(\delta)$. This excludes quotas $0$ and $n$ from the window. For the $K_i$ factor, each term in the denominator's exact log-curvature product differs from the corresponding numerator term by $O(i^{-2}+(n-i)^{-2})$. Summation gives $O(d^{-1})$.

**Liebenau–Wormald.** The packet checks the June 2020 preprint, which indeed has the stated hypotheses and error in Theorem 1.1. The 2023 version of record uses a different aspect condition and error in Theorem 1.1, equations (3)–(5). It gives the count in Remark 1.2. I checked the journal conditions directly. Set its part sizes to $(\ell,n_{\mathrm{LW}})=(n,d)$, density to $p=\min(x,1-x)$, edge count to $pnd$, and parameter $\kappa_{\mathrm{LW}}=1/20$. We have $d\le n$, $d\gg n/\log n$, and $p\gg1/\log n$. The expression in its aspect condition, after division by $p^{1/4}$, satisfies

$$
p^{-1/4}\left(\frac{n}{d^{5/4}}+\frac{d}{n^{5/4}}\right)
=O(n^{-1/4}\log^{3/2}n)=o(1).
$$

For each fixed $K>0$, $(\log^K d)/d+(\log^K n)/n=o(p)$. The row spread $d^{1/2+\eta}$ is $o((pd)^{1/2+\kappa_{\mathrm{LW}}})$ when $0<\eta<1/20$. The column spread is zero. The error in (5) is bounded by the displayed aspect term plus $O(n^{-1/2}\log^{5/2}n)$. Thus the claimed $O(n^{-1/5})$ bound holds uniformly with the journal theorem. In Remark 1.2 the column variance vanishes and the row variance is $R/n$, yielding exactly $-1/2+R/(2ndx(1-x))$ in the exponent. Complementation when $x>1/2$ preserves $R$ and the binomial prefactors. I recommend using the journal hypotheses in the enumeration appendix. [Journal Theorem 1.1 and Remark 1.2](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rsa.21105) · [2020 arXiv Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.15797#S1.SS1)

**Canfield–Greenhill–McKay.** Their dimensions are $(m,n_{\mathrm{CGM}})=(n,d)$, $A=x(1-x)/2$, and the column dispersion is zero. For $p\ge\mu_0/2$, $A$ is bounded below and the admitted margins are positive for large $n$. Both dimension restrictions follow from $d\gg n/\log n$ for any fixed permissible small exponent. The density-condition left side is $O(1+n/d)=o(\log d)$ since $\delta\log n\to\infty$. The two constants can be fixed before choosing the exponent, giving a negative-power error. The density ranges overlap. At a failed node in the linear-dimension input both the aspect ratio and density are in fixed compact ranges away from the respective boundaries, so the same checks apply. [Canfield–Greenhill–McKay, §1, Theorem 1](https://web.maths.unsw.edu.au/~csg/papers/CGM.pdf#page=2)

The quantitative summation correctly controls irregular profiles under both measures. The actual single-row law is proportional to $\binom ds A_s$. Applying the mixed-quota inequality gives $A_{s+1}^2\ge A_sA_{s+2}$, and deletion shows that the positive support is an interval. Both row laws have mean $dK/n$, and their ratios to the binomial with that mean are log-concave on their supports. Subtracting a secant of a convex test function across the interval of positive differences proves the convex-order comparison and the resulting exponential moment bounds. The actual array tail can also be checked directly by conditioning on all but two rows and using the symmetrically truncated binomial split.

The sum of the candidate rows has an interval-supported log-concave law by convolution. Jensen applied to the linear interpolation of its log mass at the integral mean gives a probability at least $1/(nd+1)$. The uniform exponential moment gives an $\exp(-c n^{1/3})$ deviation bound for the unconditioned empirical squared deviations at tolerance $n^{-1/3}$. The exponential weight has bounded second moment since its parameter is $O(\log n)$ and its leading exponent is bounded by the variance comparison. Cauchy–Schwarz followed by division by the conditioning probability still makes the discarded weighted tails negligible. This yields the relative error $O(E_n+n^{-1/3}/\delta)$, a negative power of $n$. Exact saturation is deterministic and is included. The final sentence of [quantitative-margins.md](quantitative-margins.md) should attribute the remaining limitation to CGM alone. The Liebenau–Wormald estimate supplies that range in this package.

## Variance and sign

The uniform lattice lemma holds for interval-supported log-concave laws with variance at least one. The modal mass estimates give the centered third moment bound and hence $d\sigma/d\mu=\mathbb E(X-\mu)^3/(2\sigma^3)=O(1)$. A block of sites with probability $\gg1/\sigma$ yields the Fourier decay by using distances between $M/8$ and $M/4$ for $|t|\le2\pi/M$ and the sine quotient beyond that. For bounded $\sigma$, the second moment about the mode is bounded by a constant times the sum of neighboring masses, so the mode and one neighbor bound the entire Fourier interval. Summation by parts gives the additional inverse-frequency bound. The integral of the difference of the order $n$ powers on $|t|\le a/\sigma$ is $O((n\sigma)^{-1})$. The other frequency bands give exponentially small errors with a factor $\sigma^{-1}$. This proves the relative central probability estimate $O(n^{-1/2})$ without a bound on the growth of the variance.

For $\sigma_k<n^{2/5}$, the derivative bound confines $\sigma$ on adjacent feasible quotas to $O(n^{2/5})$. The entropy contributes log-curvature $\gg n^{1/5}\delta^2$, which dominates the worst $O(\log n)$ contribution from the other terms. The central half of the triangular integral suffices, including when the later endpoint is exactly saturated. For $\sigma_k\ge n^{2/5}$ the same derivative bound keeps the entire window interior. Entropy has second derivative $-g+O(n^{-1/5}(J+2))$. The log lattice probability varies by $O(n^{-1/2}+\delta(J+2)/n^{2/5})$. In differentiating $B_i$, the variance-derivative contribution per unit quota is $O(\delta/\sqrt{dx(1-x)})=O(n^{-1/2})$, and the density-derivative contribution is $O(1/(nx(1-x)))=O(d^{-1})$. The resulting log-curvature error is a fixed negative power of $n$ throughout the window.

At either offset the log magnitude uses the central curvature with weight $j/2$ and shifted curvatures with weights $j-h$, for total $j^2/2$. The global curvature gives $e^{-\delta j^2}$ as a majorant. The sum of absolute errors in the window is $O(J^3 n^{-\kappa})$. Since $\delta J^2\gg\log^3 n$, the omitted tails are smaller than any fixed negative power. Poisson summation gives a positive main term at least $\sqrt\pi e^{-\pi^2/(8\delta)}$ using $4\delta\le g<2$. This exceeds every fixed negative power as $1/\delta=o(\log n)$, and settles the required sign despite the vanishing theta scale.

## Uniformity and the counterexample corollary

The supplied linear-dimension proof is valid. For vanishing scaled slack I checked the geometric comparison and the central probability bounds using log-concavity and entropy. For positive or infinite scaled slack I checked the uniform bracket for the truncated-normal tilt, the positive limiting variance, and the central probability bound. The linear theorem therefore excludes a subsequence with $\delta$ bounded below. Failure of the claimed uniform existential theorem would give a single sequence with $n\ge j$ and $\delta\log n\ge j$, and the estimates above exclude every such sequence.

The upper restriction $d=O(n/\log n)$ for any infinite sequence of distinct counterexamples follows, since coefficient separation rules out unbounded $d$ for bounded $n$. The newly supplied fixed-dimension argument handles every proper cap. I checked its endpoint limits and the endpoint indexing, two-saddle dominance and overlap estimates, and the root count used in its eventual real-rootedness assertion. Its use of positive denominator parameters in the hyper-Bessel zero theorem is valid. [Baricz–Singh, Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.00626)

The lower restriction $d\to\infty$ also needs the full-cap case $r=d$, since the cited fixed-dimension theorem assumes $r<d$. For full cap the polynomial is $\sum_k\binom nk^d q^k$. The sequence $\binom nk$, extended by zero for $k>n$, has exponential generating polynomial $L_n^{(0)}(-q)$ with negative zeros, so Pólya–Schur makes it a multiplier sequence. Iterating this coefficient multiplier from $(1+q)^n$ proves real-rootedness for every full cap. Add this argument or an equivalent citation. Also specify that the infinite sequence consists of counterexamples to the original real-rootedness conjecture. At $d=r=1$, $(1+q)^n$ has repeated roots for $n\ge2$. [Pólya–Schur characterization, Borcea–Brändén, Theorem 1](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/wp-content/uploads/annals-v170-n1-p14-p.pdf) · [Laguerre expansion](https://dlmf.nist.gov/18.5.E12) · [Laguerre zeros](https://dlmf.nist.gov/18.16.E9)

I also checked the Gauss–Lucas repair suggested after my initial report. If $F(x)=\sum_k f_k x^k$ has degree $n$ and strictly negative roots and $\Im x>0$, the $z$-roots of $(1+z)^n z^nF(x/z)$ lie at $-1$ or strictly below the real axis. Their convex hull meets the real axis only at $-1$. Iterating Gauss–Lucas shows that the derivative of order $n$ is nonzero at zero. Its value is $n!$ times the desired coefficient $\sum_k\binom nk f_k x^k$. Real coefficients and positivity then give strictly negative roots, and iteration from $(1+x)^n$ proves the required full-cap claim.
