## Gamma ratio and probability laws

For $s=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor$ and $a_i=(s+i)/d$, the sum of the $a_i$ is $s+(d+1)/2$. Thus $\kappa=(d-1)/2-s$ as required, with values $0$ and $1/2$ for odd and even $d$, respectively. Differentiating the logarithm of the gamma ratio with respect to the real variable $m\ge0$ gives $-\int_0^\infty e^{-my}K(y)\,dy$. Integrating in $m$ supplies the minus sign and the factor $(1-e^{-my})/y$. Jensen's inequality gives $\sum_i e^{-a_i y}\ge d e^{-y}$ because the mean of the $a_i$ is at most one. Near zero, the numerator equals $\kappa y+O(y^2)$. At infinity the kernel decays at least as fast as $e^{-a_1y}$. These bounds justify the subordinator construction and the normalized moment formula. The large-jump part also admits the exponential moment at $b=s/d$, since $a_1-b=1/d$. For the small jumps, $(e^{by}-1)/y$ is bounded. The ratio of exponential moments defining the tilted Laplace transform then replaces $K$ by $K_b=e^{by}K$ exactly.

The gamma multiplication formula at $m+(s+1)/d$ gives a power of $d$ equal to $1/2-d(m+(s+1)/d)=-dm-s-1/2$. The gamma argument is $dm+s+1$. Term $m$ of $e^{bX}E_{d,s}(dUe^{-X/d})$ contains the factor $e^{bX-(s+dm)X/d}=e^{-mX}$. After multiplying by the displayed prefactor and taking expectations, its coefficient is therefore $(-1)^mU^{dm}/(m!)^d$. The sum of the absolute expectations equals $F_d(U^d)$ after the same positive scaling, so the expectation commutes with the series.

For $0<y\le1$, apply $e^{-x}\le1-x+x^2/2$ to the positive numerator terms and $e^{-x}\ge1-x$ to the subtracted term. The numerator is bounded by $\kappa y+(y^2/2)\sum_i(i/d)^2\le\kappa y+dy^2/2$. Applying $(1-e^{-y})^{-1}\le1+1/y$ gives the stated $\kappa+(d+1)y$ bound. For $y\ge1$, the denominator has reciprocal at most $2$, and the numerator is bounded by both $de^{-y/d}$ and $(e^{y/d}-1)^{-1}\le d/y$. Integrating $e^{-qy}K_b(y)$ using these bounds gives all three terms in the tilted mean. In the integral for $-\log L_b(T)$, split the constant small-jump contribution at $1/(1+T)$ and apply $1-e^{-Ty}\le\min(Ty,1)$. Its upper bound is $\kappa(1+\log(1+T))$. The other two contributions are at most $d+1$ and $2d$. This verifies the claimed Laplace bound.

## Principal phase and remaining labels

The [upper-edge filter](angular-upper-edge-sign.md) applies for every real argument of $E_{d,s}$. The terms at $\pm\theta$ give the principal cosine with argument $dU Z\sin\theta-s\theta$. Since $dU\sin\theta=k\pi+(d-1)\theta/2$, multiplying by $(-1)^k$ leaves exactly $\cos(\kappa\theta-dU\sin\theta(1-Z))$. The amplitude $2M/d$ and the normalization of the $b$-tilt are positive.

After cancellation of $e^{dT}$, the principal law weights the $b$-tilted law by $e^{-g(X)}$ where $g(X)=dT(1-e^{-X/d})$. Its denominator is at least $L_b(T)$ since $g(X)\le TX$. Off $Z\ge c$, the numerator is at most $e^{-dT(1-c)}$, which proves the stated complement bound. On $Z\ge c$, the conditional likelihood ratio to the linear $q=cT$ tilt is proportional to $e^{-g(X)+qX}$ and decreases with $X$. For a real random variable $H$ and decreasing weight $w$, independence of two copies gives $2\mathrm{Cov}(H,w(H))=\mathbb E[(H-H')(w(H)-w(H'))]\le0$. Applying this on the retained interval gives the comparison of conditional means. The unconditional linear tilted mean is larger because the interval ends at $X=-d\log c$. The Lipschitz cosine bound uses $dU\sin\theta(1-Z)\le U\sin\theta\,X$. Comparing the cosine with $\cos(\kappa\theta)$ on the complement costs at most $2\tau$.

Under the same law, a nonprincipal label at $\beta$ has modulus at most $\tfrac12\mathbb E\exp(-dUZ(\cos\theta-\cos\beta))$ in normalized units. The factor $1/2$ is from the two principal labels. For the positive representative $\beta=(2\ell+1)\theta\le\pi$, the identity $\cos\theta-\cos\beta=2\sin(\ell\theta)\sin((\ell+1)\theta)$ gives $8\ell^2/d^2$ by the sine inequality. The supplement of $(\ell+1)\theta$ is at least $\ell\theta$ when $2\ell+1\le d$, so the estimate covers the odd-dimensional singleton at $\pi$. The representatives each account for at most two labels. The complement thus costs at most $(d-2)\tau/2$, so the larger stated $d\tau$ bound is valid.

## Dimension two and the finite strip

Expanding the integral $J_0(t)=\pi^{-1}\int_0^\pi\cos(t\cos u)\,du$ gives $F_2(-U^2)=J_0(2U)$. For $y(t)=\sqrt t\,J_0(t)$, the power series gives $y''+(1+1/(4t^2))y=0$. Its vector $(y,-y')$ is nowhere zero on $t>0$ by uniqueness. Write it as $r(\cos\psi,\sin\psi)$ with $r>0$. Differentiation gives $\psi'=1+\cos^2\psi/(4t^2)$. In the integral and its derivative, the two endpoint stationary-phase terms give $(y,-y')=\sqrt{2/\pi}(\cos(t-\pi/4),\sin(t-\pi/4))+o(1)$. This fixes a continuous lift with $\psi-(t-\pi/4)\to0$. Integrating the derivative correction yields a discrepancy between $-1/(4t)$ and $0$. At $t=\pi(k+1/4)$ the reference angle is $k\pi$, and the discrepancy has magnitude less than $\pi/2$, so the sign is strict.

For $3\le d\le7$, the complement is $\lfloor d/4\rfloor+1\le k\le100d-1$, giving $2491$ pairs. For $8\le d\le127$, it is $\lfloor d/4\rfloor+1\le k\le127$, giving $13260$ pairs. For $d\ge128$ the adjacent and angular regimes meet at $k=d/4$ and cover every integer $k\ge1$.
