# Independent referee report on the balanced-array corank-one argument

Agent-assisted review, 2026-11-25. I reviewed the anonymized argument and the original conjecture.

## Verdict

The argument proves real-rootedness for every $n\geq0$, $d\geq2$ and $r=d-1$, as well as simplicity and negativity of all roots and strict consecutive interlacing for nonconstant pairs. I found no mathematical gap in this restricted theorem. The original conjecture asserts real-rootedness for all $1\leq r\leq d$ and $n\geq0$. Its highlighted instance $(r,d)=(2,4)$ lies outside the proved family. The strict interlacing conclusion strengthens real-rootedness within the proved family. [Primary conjecture, “Balanced arrays of uniform matroids”](https://www.symmetricfunctions.com/realRootedOpenProblems.htm#conjBalancedArrays)

## Verification of the proof

**Counting and normalization.** Choosing a specified set of $j$ full rows leaves $d$ independent choices of $k-j$ positions in $n-j$ rows. Inclusion–exclusion therefore gives the claimed count. With $m=n-j$ and $h=k-j$, the signed contribution to $[z^n q^k]V$ is

$$
(-1)^j\binom nj\binom{n-j}{k-j}^{d}\frac{(n-k)!^d}{n!}
=\frac{(-1)^j m!^{d-1}}{j!(h!)^d}.
$$

This gives $V=e^{-qz}W$. The coefficient of $z^n$ in $W-z(\theta+1)^{d-1}W$ is $q^n/n!$. Conjugating $\theta+1$ by $e^{qz}$ gives $\theta+1+qz$. In the basis $1,z,\dots,z^{n-1}$, truncated modulo $z^n$, its matrix is $D+qL$ with the stated $1$-based diagonal. For $q\neq0$, diagonal conjugation by $\mathrm{diag}(1,q,\dots,q^{n-1})$ gives the stated powers of $q$ in the entries of $(D+qL)^{d-1}$. The relation extends to $q=0$ as a polynomial identity.

**Determinant identity.** Deleting row $n$ and column $j$ of $A-qU$ leaves a block lower triangular minor. Its top left block is the size-$j-1$ leading block of $A-qU$, and its bottom right block is lower triangular with its $n-j$ diagonal entries equal to $-q$. The cofactor sign $(-1)^{n+j}$ cancels the sign in $(-q)^{n-j}$. Including $\det(\varnothing)=1$, this proves the same recurrence as for $v_n$. The alternating-sign conjugation sends $A-qU$ to $(D-L)^{d-1}+qU$, whose determinant divided by $(n!)^{d-1}$ is $\det(I+qC_nU)$. A direct exact check generated arrays by adding every permissible binary row to a table of margin counts. For $2\le d\le5$ and $0\le n\le7$, the resulting balanced coefficients agreed with inclusion–exclusion in all 32 cases. The normalized polynomials agreed with the Hessenberg determinants at all $n+1$ integer inputs $0,\dots,n$ in each case, totaling 144 exact rational determinant evaluations.

**Oscillatory matrix hypotheses.** The formula for $(D-L)^{-1}$ follows by solving each column of the lower bidiagonal system. The prefix-sum matrix equals $(I+E_{n,n-1})\cdots(I+E_{2,1})$. Each factor is totally nonnegative, as is the nonnegative upper bidiagonal $U+\epsilon I$. Cauchy–Binet gives total nonnegativity for $C_n(U+\epsilon I)$. Its determinant is $\epsilon^n/(n!)^{d-1}$, its first superdiagonal entries are $(C_n)_{ii}>0$, and its first subdiagonal entries are $(C_n)_{i+1,i-1}+\epsilon(C_n)_{i+1,i}>0$ with the missing column treated as zero. The lower triangular inverse powers nest. When forming an entry of the leading block of the product, lower triangularity of $C_n$ restricts the summation index to at most its row index, so the product blocks also nest. The classical oscillatory criterion therefore applies. The interlacing theorem for the matrix obtained by deleting the **last** row and column applies to exactly these consecutive blocks. The positive eigenvalues give negative zeros of $\det(xI+C_n(U+\epsilon I))$. The limit of the ordered roots preserves the weak inequalities, including coincident zeros. [Gantmacher and Krein, *Oscillation Matrices and Kernels and Small Vibrations of Mechanical Systems*](https://doi.org/10.1090/chel/345)

**Multiplier and repeated roots.** For $F$ of degree $N$, reversing with respect to $N$ gives $f(t)=\sum_iF_i t^{N-i}$, and hence $f(\partial)x^N=\sum_i F_i N!x^i/i!$. Each nonzero root $\rho$ of $F$ contributes a factor $1-\rho t$ to $f$ up to the leading constant of $F$. A root of $F$ at zero reduces $\deg f$. For a polynomial $g$ whose distinct roots are $\alpha_1<\cdots<\alpha_s$ with multiplicities $m_i$, the factor $g+\lambda g'$ for $\lambda\ne0$ leaves multiplicity exactly $m_i-1$ at $\alpha_i$. Elsewhere its roots solve $\sum_i m_i/(x-\alpha_i)=-1/\lambda$. The left side strictly decreases from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$ in each bounded gap and has ranges $(-\infty,0)$ and $(0,\infty)$ on the left and right exterior gaps, respectively. It follows that each new root is simple and that all roots are accounted for. Starting at $x^N$, any repeated root throughout this process must occur at zero. The case $N=0$ is constant. The operator thus gives simplicity away from zero even for pencils whose coefficients have varying signs.

**Interlacing and direction.** For consecutive reversed polynomials of degrees $n$ and $n+1$, weak interlacing is equivalent to real-rootedness of the pencil with every real coefficient $c$, by Obreschkoff's theorem. The term of degree $n+1$ never cancels, and the diagonal multiplier preserves both degrees. If the transformed pair $G,H$ shared a nonzero root $a$, the smaller polynomial would have $G'(a)\ne0$. The choice $c=-H'(a)/G'(a)$ would give a repeated nonzero root of the transformed pencil, contrary to the multiplier property. The argument applies after each iteration. The scaled $d$-fold transform has coefficient $a_{n,k}$ at exponent $n-k$ because $(n)_k=n!/(n-k)!$. The degree of $P_n$ is $\lfloor n(d-1)/d\rfloor$. The upper bound follows by counting ones. To attain it for $n>0$, distribute the $dk$ column entries consecutively over rows modulo $n$, with the $k$ entries of each column in a block. Since $k\le n$, the rows in each block are distinct, and any row gets at most $\lceil dk/n\rceil\le d-1$ entries. The empty array gives $P_0=1$. [Savage and Visontai, Theorem 6.1, citing Obreschkoff's Satz 5.2](https://dmtcs.episciences.org/articles/2321/download)

Write $a_1<\cdots<a_m<0$ and $b_1<\cdots<b_{m'}<0$ for the nonzero roots of the smaller and larger reversed polynomials, where the sizes refer to $n$ and $n+1$. For $m'=m+1$, the inequalities alternate as $b_1<a_1<b_2<\cdots<b_m<a_m<b_{m+1}$. For $m'=m$, the same inequalities end with $b_m<a_m<0$, because the next root of the larger reversed polynomial is zero. The reciprocal map reverses order on the negative half-line. In either case the roots in $q$ strictly interlace and $1/b_1>1/a_1$, so the new polynomial has its rightmost root closer to zero.
